Blog 1: A Phenomenon in My World

Generic Medication Failures

I plan to write my paper about differences in generic stimulant medications not being taken seriously as a drug that is necessary for the patient, unlike a different class of likewise controlled substances that are accepted in society.

I received pink instead of orange Adderall at the pharmacy for the third time this year. Each time has been devastating, but each time has not been as equally as infuriating as the third. After getting it filled again, I realized I did not feel like myself and could not concentrate, which made me feel like my day was completely off.

The pink Adderall is a completely different medication that comes with far less benefits to executive function and with side-effects like anxiety. As a controlled substance, I did what I could to manage the issue, but the experience made me realize how often generic stimulant medications are treated as less important despite the very serious impact they can have on daily life.

I was able to get a new order of the regular Adderall I take after a week of waiting for my psychiatrist appointment, but this would not have happened unless I was working with a psychiatrist who recognized how essential my prescription was to my functioning.

Viewing stimulant medications as a class of drugs that is less serious than others seems to have a history that dates back to a Ritalin advertisement in 1948 in a popular magazine “The Saturday Evening Post,” where the drug was marketed as a treatment for “difficult children,” which shaped public ideas of ADHD medications in a less medically serious way.

So far my tentative thesis statement is while it’s likely that stimulant drugs are not taken as seriously as other controlled substances due to different safety profiles, it is also possible that the issue is rooted in the way stimulant medications have been culturally constructed as less severe or less dangerous.
